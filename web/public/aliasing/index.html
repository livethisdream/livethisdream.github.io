Aliasing: the Wagon Wheel

A camera samples a spinning wheel at fs frames per second. It only ever sees where the red spoke is at each frame, so any rotation that ends each frame interval at the same place looks identical. Spin the wheel faster than fs/2 and the camera reports a different, slower rotation, sometimes backwards. The spectrum below shows why: sampling copies the wheel's frequency every fs, and what we see is the copy that lands between −fs/2 and +fs/2.

What to look for

Below fs/2 nothing is lost. The camera's wheel turns the same way at the same speed, and the red copy in the spectrum is the true line itself.

Between fs/2 and fs the wheel runs backwards. Each frame the spoke turns more than half a turn, so the shortest way to the next position is the other way round. In the spectrum the copy at f0 − fs is the one inside the shaded band, and it is negative.

At exactly fs the wheel freezes, because it makes one full turn per frame. At exactly fs/2 the direction is ambiguous: the spoke flips back and forth.

Rotation versus shadow. The rotation is a complex exponential, ej2πf0t: it has one spectral line, and its sign says which way the wheel turns. Its alias wraps: f0 minus the nearest multiple of fs. The shadow on the floor, cos(2πf0t), is the real part. It has lines at both +f0 and −f0 and cannot tell direction, so its alias folds back and forth across 0 and fs/2, like an accordion. This is why a real-sampling receiver needs its input below fs/2, while an I/Q (complex) receiver can use the whole span from −fs/2 to +fs/2.
